A good QBR deck leaves the customer with three things: proof that last quarter was worth paying for, agreement on what next quarter should achieve, and a few slides their champion can forward to the executive who signs the renewal.

Most QBR decks deliver none of these. They are usage reports with a logo on them, copied forward from the last quarter. Here is how to build one that works.

## What a QBR is for

A quarterly business review is not a product update and not a status report. It exists to:

1. **Show the value delivered**, measured against the goals the customer set, in their language.
2. **Align on the next quarter**: what they want to achieve and what both sides will do about it.
3. **Strengthen the relationship above your day-to-day contact**, so the renewal decision is made by someone who has seen the results.

If the deck does not do the first job, the other two do not happen. Start there.

## Know who is in the room, and who is not

A QBR usually has two audiences.

- **Your champion**, the person you work with every week. They know the details and want help making the case internally.
- **The executive sponsor**, who may or may not attend. They care about outcomes, cost, and risk, and they will give the deck about ninety seconds.

Build for the executive and let the champion fill in the detail. Every key slide should make sense forwarded on its own, with no narration, to someone who was not in the meeting. If the executive skips the meeting, a forwarded slide is the only version of the QBR they will see.

## The structure that works

Ten to twelve slides, plus an appendix.

1. **Cover and objectives.** The customer's logo next to yours, the date, and what this meeting is meant to decide.
2. **Executive summary.** One slide. The value delivered this quarter in one sentence, the headline results, and the one or two things you want to agree on today.
3. **Their goals, recapped.** What they told you they wanted to achieve, in their words, from the last QBR or from the kickoff. This is the yardstick for everything that follows.
4. **Results against those goals.** Outcomes, not activity. "Proposal turnaround down from 5 days to 2" is a result. "3,400 documents created" is activity. Put the numbers that map to their goals on this slide, and move the rest to the appendix.
5. **Adoption and usage.** Which teams are using it, where adoption is strong, where it is lagging, and why. Usage matters because it predicts renewal, but it supports the results slide, not the other way around.
6. **What did not go well.** Support issues, missed milestones, anything they have complained about. Name it before they do, with what you have done about it.
7. **Recommendations.** Two or three specific things that would get them more value next quarter: a team to roll out to, a feature they are not using, a workflow to change.
8. **Roadmap, filtered.** Only the upcoming product changes that matter to this customer. This is not the place for your full roadmap.
9. **Next quarter's goals and the plan.** Goals agreed in the meeting, with owners on both sides and dates.
10. **Appendix.** Detailed usage tables, support ticket history, anything the champion might need to answer a question later.

## Make the value slide work

The results slide carries the whole QBR, so it deserves the most effort.

- **Use their metrics.** If they bought you to shorten sales cycles, report sales cycle length, not logins.
- **Show before and after.** A baseline from before they started, or from last quarter, next to the current figure.
- **Translate into money or time when you can.** "Saves each rep about four hours a week, roughly 1,600 hours a quarter across the team" is something an executive can repeat in a budget meeting.
- **Pull the headline numbers forward.** Big figures, clearly labeled, with the chart or table beneath them. A customer should be able to scan the slide in ten seconds and know how the quarter went.
- **Be honest about attribution.** If other factors contributed, say so. Overclaiming is the fastest way to lose the executive's trust.

## Common mistakes

- **Making it about your product.** Feature tours and release notes belong in a separate session. The QBR is about their outcomes.
- **Reporting activity instead of results.** Logins and documents created prove the tool is used, not that it is worth paying for.
- **Copying last quarter's deck forward.** Stale sections, an old template and last year's colors tell the customer exactly how much attention the account gets.
- **Springing the renewal or expansion ask.** If there is a commercial conversation coming, set it up in the recommendations and next steps, and make sure the champion knows it is coming.
- **No agreed next steps.** A QBR that ends without owners and dates is a presentation, not a review.
