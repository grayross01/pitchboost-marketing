A good board presentation gets a decision made in the meeting instead of a follow-up call three weeks later. Directors arrive already knowing the numbers, spend the meeting on the two or three questions that matter, and leave having said yes or no.

That outcome depends less on polish than on structure. Here is the structure that works, how to present the numbers, and how to design slides that survive being read on a phone in an airport lounge.

## What a board presentation is for

A board presentation has three jobs, in this order:

1. **Get decisions made.** Approve the budget, the hire, the raise, the pivot, the new market.
2. **Surface what the board needs to know.** Especially the bad news, early and plainly.
3. **Report performance.** The numbers against plan.

Most decks invert this. They open with twenty minutes of metrics and reach the decision with ten minutes left and half the room checking email. Put the decisions first, and treat the reporting as the evidence behind them.

## Send it as a pre-read

The deck is usually read before the meeting, not presented in it. Send it three to five days ahead. That gives directors time to read, form questions, and call you with anything sensitive before it comes up in front of everyone.

That changes how you build it. A pre-read has to make sense without you in the room. Every slide needs a title that states its point, and every chart needs enough labeling to read without a narrator. If a slide only works when you explain it, rewrite the slide.

In the meeting itself, you are not walking through the deck page by page. You take the decisions and discuss what the directors raise. The deck is the reference.

## The structure that works

For a quarterly board meeting at a startup or a small company, 10 to 15 slides plus an appendix is plenty.

1. **Cover and agenda.** Meeting date, and the decisions you need from this meeting listed up front.
2. **Executive summary.** One slide. The three to five things a director should know if they read nothing else: how the quarter went against plan, the biggest risk, what you are asking for. If you only get one slide right, make it this one.
3. **Decisions requested.** Each decision on its own slide: the ask, the options you considered, your recommendation, what it costs, and what happens if the answer is no.
4. **Key metrics against plan.** The same five to eight metrics every meeting, each shown against plan and against the prior period. Consistency matters more than the choice of metrics: directors compare this deck with the last one.
5. **Financials.** Revenue, burn or profit, and cash, with runway in months if you are a startup. Actuals against budget, with a one-line explanation for any variance that matters.
6. **What went well, what did not.** Be specific and be early with bad news. Directors forgive misses. They do not forgive surprises.
7. **Strategy and key initiatives.** Where the big projects stand, and anything that changes the plan.
8. **Risks.** The few that could actually hurt, what you are doing about each, and where you need help.
9. **Appendix.** Detailed financials, departmental updates, pipeline, hiring. Everything a director might want to check, without putting it in the main flow.

Nonprofit boards follow the same pattern with different metrics: programs and outcomes in place of growth, restricted and unrestricted funds in place of burn.

## Presenting the numbers

Board members read numbers comparatively. A figure on its own means nothing. A figure against plan, against last quarter, and against last year tells a story.

- **Always show the comparison.** Actual, plan, variance. Or this quarter against the last four.
- **Explain every variance that matters, in one line.** "Revenue 8% below plan: two enterprise deals slipped to October, both signed since."
- **Round.** $2.4M, not $2,413,882.17. The appendix can carry the decimals.
- **Keep definitions stable.** If "active customer" meant something different last quarter, say so on the slide. Nothing loses a board's trust faster than a metric that quietly changed shape.
- **One chart, one point.** Title the chart with the takeaway, not the label: "Burn down 20% while revenue grew 15%" rather than "Burn and revenue, Q1 to Q3". Our guide to [fixing a broken chart](/blog/fix-broken-chart-in-powerpoint) covers making charts readable.

## Designing slides directors can read

Directors read board packs on phones, tablets and laptops, often between other commitments. That puts a floor under the design.

- **Titles that state the point.** "Cash runway extends to 22 months after the bridge" beats "Cash position".
- **Nothing smaller than 14 point**, including table text and chart labels. If a table does not fit at that size, it belongs in the appendix.
- **One message per slide.** Two points on one slide means one of them gets missed.
- **Consistent layout every quarter.** Metrics in the same place, in the same order, in the same format. Directors should be able to flip between this quarter's deck and last quarter's and compare slide by slide.
- **Current branding.** A deck that still carries the pre-rebrand logo and last year's colors signals that nobody has looked at it closely, which is exactly the wrong message for a governance document.

Board decks tend to drift badly because each quarter starts from a copy of the last one. Tables get pasted from finance in a different font, a new section gets added in a different style, and after a year the deck is a patchwork. ## Common mistakes

- **Leading with metrics instead of decisions.** The ask ends up on slide 14 with five minutes left.
- **Hiding bad news in the appendix.** Someone will find it, and the conversation becomes about why it was hidden.
- **Changing the metrics every quarter.** The board cannot tell whether things are getting better.
- **Sending it the night before.** Nobody reads it, and the meeting becomes a live read-through.
- **Forty slides.** Length is not rigor. Move detail to the appendix.
