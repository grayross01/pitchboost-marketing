You have finished the thesis. Now you have thirty to sixty minutes to present it, twenty to thirty of those in slides, with a committee that has read the abstract, skimmed the middle and will spend the rest of the time asking questions. The slides are the map they follow while you talk.

Most defense decks are the same slide problem repeated twenty-five times: too much text, an advisor template from 2011, a font that does not exist on the projector, one chart per slide that nobody in the back can read. The content is a year of work. The presentation looks like it took an afternoon.

Here is what actually goes on each slide, how many slides you need, and how to get a deck that looks like the work you did.

## How long should a thesis defense presentation be?

For most Master's and PhD programs, the presentation portion runs 20 to 45 minutes, with 20 to 30 minutes being the common range. Ask your program specifically. A one-hour defense typically means 25 minutes of talk and 35 minutes of questions.

The slide count depends on how you use slides, not on the length of the talk. A safe range: **12 to 20 content slides for a 20 to 30 minute talk**, plus a title slide and a references slide. If you are past 25 content slides you are probably reading them.

## What slides does a thesis defense need?

The exact structure varies by field, but almost every defense deck contains these sections in some order:

1. **Title slide.** Thesis title, your name, advisor and committee, institution, defense date. This is on screen while people take seats; make it readable.
2. **Motivation.** The problem, in one slide. Why does this question matter, and to whom. If the committee cannot state your research question after this slide, nothing that follows will land.
3. **Background and prior work.** Two to four slides. Enough for a committee member from a nearby field to follow. Do not re-teach the field to the people who wrote it.
4. **Research question and hypothesis.** One slide, stated cleanly. Everything after this slide is either evidence for or against.
5. **Methods.** Two to five slides depending on how much depends on the method. Diagrams beat prose. If your method is the contribution, spend more time here.
6. **Results.** The largest section, usually five to eight slides. One finding per slide. Show the data, then tell the audience what it means underneath.
7. **Discussion.** Two to three slides connecting results back to the research question. This is the part committees remember.
8. **Limitations.** One slide. Do not skip this. If you do not name the limits, the committee will, and you will be defending the omission.
9. **Contributions and future work.** One slide, sometimes two. What is new because of this thesis. What comes next.
10. **Thank you / questions.** Your contact and any acknowledgments.

An appendix of extra slides (extra results, methodological detail, robustness checks) is standard and useful. Committee members will ask questions that map to your appendix; when they do, jump straight to that slide.

## How many words per slide?

If you are reading the slide, the audience is reading it too, and neither of you is listening. A defense slide should have:

- One title that states the finding, not the topic. "Method X improves accuracy by 12%" beats "Results".
- One chart, one diagram, one equation, or three to six bullets. Not all of them.
- Numbers that a person in the back row can read without leaning forward.

If a slide has three paragraphs, split it into three slides, or move two paragraphs to speaker notes. The audience should be able to read every slide in five seconds and then look at you.

## The structural moves that separate a strong defense deck

A few small structural choices make a bigger difference than any template.

**Put the finding in the slide title.** "Ridge regression outperforms baseline across all three datasets" is a slide title. "Model comparison" is a placeholder. A committee that reads only your titles should walk away with your argument.

**Put the reading of the data underneath the chart, not next to it.** A chart on the top two-thirds of the slide, with a single sentence beneath it saying what the chart shows and what it means, is the layout most defense slides should default to.

**Show your data once, then reference it.** If figure 3 appears again in the discussion, reuse the same figure. The committee learns your figures. Do not surprise them with a new visualization of the same data.

**Number your contributions.** If you have three contributions, put "Three contributions" on the discussion slide with 1, 2, 3, and refer back to them. When a committee member asks a broad question, you can answer by pointing at the numbered list.

**Practice the transitions, not the slides.** The words between two slides are the ones you will forget. Rehearse "which brings us to..." not "on this next slide you can see...".

## How to make a defense deck look serious without spending a week

Most defense decks look homemade because they are: pieced together over the last week from an advisor's template, a couple of Overleaf figures, and slides someone else made three years ago. The result is usable but obviously unfinished.

Two paths get you to a real-looking deck.

**Path 1: fix by hand.** Pick one font family and use it everywhere. Pick two accent colors (usually your institution's colors) and use them for headings and chart accents. Standardize the position of the title, the position of the page number, and the size of body text. Do this on the first three slides, then apply the same rules to the rest. ## A note on charts, tables and equations

Defense committees look at your data very carefully. A few rules that apply almost universally:

- **Every chart has axes labeled with words, not variable names.** "Loss (cross entropy)" not "loss_ce".
- **Every table caption states the finding.** "Table 2. Model A beats Model B on all held-out sets." Not "Table 2. Comparison of models."
- **Equations belong on their own line and get a number if you refer to them later.** LaTeX-rendered equations in an image look better than PowerPoint's equation editor for anything more complex than a fraction.
- **If you have color-coded results, use a color scheme that survives grayscale printing.** The committee may print your slides.

## What to send to the committee before the defense

Most programs require slides submitted 24 to 48 hours before the defense. Two useful things you can do here:

1. Send a link to the deck instead of only the file, and check who opens it and how long they spend on which section. A shareable link tells you which slides the committee is already looking at, so you can decide which sections to lean into during the talk. This is the difference between sending a .pptx attachment and sending a trackable link with [PitchBoost's viewer analytics](/features/viewer-analytics).
2. Send a one-page summary alongside the deck. Committees who have skimmed a one-pager arrive with sharper questions and a better sense of what your defense is about.

## The short version

A defense deck is not a summary of your thesis. It is a 20 to 30 minute argument, told in 12 to 20 content slides, with one finding per slide, one chart per finding, and the finding stated in the slide title. Motivation, background, question, methods, results, discussion, limitations, contributions. An appendix of extra results behind the last slide, ready for questions.

The deck should look like it belongs to the same document. If your last hour before the defense is spent aligning text boxes, either pick one font and stop, or rebuild the deck with a tool that applies one design across every slide.
