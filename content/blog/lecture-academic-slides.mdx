Lecture slides sit in a strange corner of presentation design. They have to work in the room, where students should be listening to you and not reading. They have to work later, as review material, when students study the deck without you next to it. And they have to work every semester, when you drop in a new example and cut an old one, without the whole deck slowly turning into someone else's template.

That is a harder brief than a conference talk. A conference deck has one job on one day. Lecture slides have three jobs across a whole term.

Most academic lecture decks split the difference badly. They are too dense to be good live slides and too fragmented to be good study material. Here is how to design lecture slides that do both.

## What lecture slides are actually for

Two audiences, both real:

1. **The student in the room, right now.** They should be listening to you, not reading. If they are transcribing your slide, the slide is fighting you.
2. **The student two weeks later, revising for the exam.** They do not have you next to them. They have the deck, maybe a recording, and their notes.

Live-only slides (a conference deck) can be sparse because the talk carries the content. Study-only slides (a textbook) can be dense because there is no talk. Lecture slides have to give the live audience a signal to follow and give the studying student enough context to reconstruct the argument.

The trick is not writing dense slides that satisfy both. It is designing sparse slides in the room and a fuller companion version for study, from the same source.

## How many slides for an hour of lecture?

For a 50-minute lecture, plan for **20 to 35 content slides**, plus a title slide, one or two agenda slides and a summary. That is roughly 90 seconds to 2.5 minutes per slide.

Fewer if you spend real time on each slide (working through a derivation, walking through a case). More if slides are quick anchors while you talk. Whatever the number, do not fill a 50-minute slot with 60 dense slides. You will read them and the students will stop listening.

## What belongs on a lecture slide

Three things, in order:

1. **A title that states the point of the slide.** "Selection bias inflates observed correlations" beats "Selection bias".
2. **One visual or one small piece of evidence.** A chart, a diagram, a code snippet, one worked example.
3. **Two to five short bullets, or one paragraph of a sentence or two.** Only what the audience needs to see to follow the visual.

If a slide has five bullets and a chart and a table, it is three slides. Split it. Split slides are cheaper than confused students.

The extras (references, exceptions, deeper derivations) belong in the study version of the deck, not the live one.

## Structure for a typical lecture

Not every subject fits the same shape, but a workable default:

1. **Title slide.** Course number, lecture number, date, topic. This is on screen while students arrive.
2. **Where we are in the course.** One slide that reminds them what was covered last time and what fits with today. Two sentences.
3. **What you will learn today.** Three or four learning objectives. Concrete verbs (compute, distinguish, apply), not vague ones (understand, appreciate).
4. **The core content.** The middle of the lecture. Structured by concept, not by slide count. Each concept gets: an introducing slide, one or two worked examples, and a slide that connects the concept to the objective.
5. **A worked problem or case.** One extended example that ties the concepts together. This is the slide sequence students will re-read the most.
6. **Common mistakes.** One slide of what students usually get wrong on this topic. Students remember this slide.
7. **Summary.** One slide restating the three or four things they should walk out with. Mirror the learning objectives.
8. **What is next.** One slide previewing next lecture, plus reading or problem set.

Skip the parts your course does not need. Do not skip the summary. Students who missed the middle of the lecture use the summary to know whether they have to catch up before the next one.

## Rules that hold across every academic deck

**One font, everywhere.** Pick a legible sans (Inter, Source Sans, Aptos, Helvetica Now) for body text and a monospace (JetBrains Mono, IBM Plex Mono) for code if the subject needs it. Serif is fine if you prefer it; the point is pick one and stop.

**Two colors, plus black and white.** A primary for headings and one accent for emphasis or contrast. Every chart, every diagram, every callout uses those two colors. If you are teaching a subject with color-coded conventions (red for wrong, green for right, blue for information), use those consistently across every slide of every lecture.

**Same layout every slide.** Title in the same place, page number in the same place, slide count in the same place. Students should be able to say "go back to slide 14" and land on the same visual position every time.

**Body text at 24pt minimum.** In a big lecture hall, 28pt. If your body text is 18pt, students in the back cannot read it, which means they stop trying.

**Chart axes labeled with words, not variables.** "Weeks after infection" not "t_w". "Effect size (Cohen's d)" not "d".

## The live-and-study split

The single biggest change most academic decks benefit from is producing two versions of the same deck from the same source.

- **Live version.** Sparse. One point per slide. Big visuals. Short bullets. Speaker notes carry the elaboration.
- **Study version.** Same slides, but the elaboration from your speaker notes is included on the slide (or on a facing page in the PDF). Students can revise from the deck without needing a recording.

The easiest way to do this is to write the lecture into speaker notes fully, then let the presentation tool export a "notes view" PDF as the study version and use the slides-only view live.

If you write on a whiteboard during the lecture, photograph the board and add the photograph to the study version too. What was ephemeral in the room becomes reference material for the exam.

## What breaks over semesters

Lecture decks decay. A course taught for years turns into a slide graveyard: three fonts, six template ancestors, examples from 2018 with data from 2023, one obviously-new slide inside a run of obviously-old ones. It happens because nobody has time to redesign the whole deck between semesters.

Two ways to fight it:

**Pin a style guide inside the course folder.** One page: font, sizes, colors, chart rules, title format. Every new slide passes this test. This does not fix the old slides but it stops the decay from spreading.

**Rebuild the deck when it drifts too far.** Once every year or two, take the current version and rebuild it against your style guide. ## Special cases

**Math-heavy lectures.** Equations get their own slide space. Number every equation you reference later. LaTeX-rendered equations look better than PowerPoint's equation editor for anything more complex than a fraction. If the derivation is long, split it across two or three slides so the audience can follow the steps.

**Case-based lectures.** Structure each case as a mini-arc: setup slide, question slide, discussion slides, resolution slide. Students remember cases they can retell. Slide structure should support the retelling.

**Lab or coding lectures.** Code slides are read, not skimmed. 10 to 15 lines maximum. Highlight the two lines that matter. Live-code separately if you can; keep the slides as the anchor students can look at.

## The short version

Lecture slides are teaching tools with two audiences: the student in the room and the student revising two weeks later. Sparse in the room, fuller for study, from the same source with speaker notes doing the heavy lifting.

One font, two colors, one layout, body text at 24pt minimum, findings in the slide titles. 20 to 35 content slides for a 50-minute lecture. A summary slide. A common-mistakes slide. A study version alongside the live version.
