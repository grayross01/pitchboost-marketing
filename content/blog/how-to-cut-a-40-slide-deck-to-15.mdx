Nobody sets out to build a 40-slide deck. It happens one addition at a time: a slide for the objection someone raised in March, three for the case study legal approved in May, a chart because a customer asked. Each made sense. Together they make a deck nobody can present in the time they are given.

Cutting it is not about deleting; it is about deciding what the deck is for and letting that decision do the deleting.

## Start with the sentence

Write one sentence: "After this deck, the audience should believe X and do Y." If you cannot write it, the deck cannot be cut, because there is nothing to cut toward. Once you have it, every slide gets one question: does this move them toward X and Y? Slides that do stay. Slides that answer a question nobody in the room will ask go to the appendix. Slides that repeat an earlier slide with a different chart go.

## The four kinds of slides in a long deck

Sorting the deck into these buckets makes the cut mechanical.

1. **Spine slides.** The argument itself: the problem, why now, what you do, proof, what happens next. Usually 8 to 12 slides. These stay and get the design attention.
2. **Proof slides.** Case studies, logos, numbers, testimonials. Keep the two strongest; move the rest to the appendix where they can be pulled up on demand.
3. **Objection slides.** Security, pricing detail, integration lists, the competitor comparison. Real questions, but not for slide 9. Appendix, with a one-line mention in the spine.
4. **Legacy slides.** The slide someone important once asked for, the org chart, the timeline from a launch that already happened. These are the ones nobody will notice are gone.

## Merge, do not just delete

The best cuts combine. Three case-study slides become one slide with three outcomes. A features slide and a benefits slide become one slide that pairs each feature with what it does for the buyer. Four slides of "our process" become one process graphic. The content survives; the slide count does not.

## What 15 slides usually looks like

A sales or investor deck cut well tends to land near this shape: title, the problem in one line, why it matters now, what you do, how it works (one slide, one graphic), proof (two slides at most), who it is for, pricing or model, the team or the company in one slide, the ask, and a closing slide. That is 11 to 13, with room for two slides specific to the audience in the room.

## Keep the long version

Do not delete the 40-slide deck. Save it as the reference or the leave-behind. The 15-slide version is for the meeting; the long version is for the reader who wants everything, and some do. Two decks with clear jobs beat one deck that tries to do both.
